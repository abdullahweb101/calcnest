What Is Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC)?

Customer Acquisition Cost, or CAC, is a business metric that measures the average amount spent to acquire one new customer. It helps companies understand how much they invest in sales and marketing activities to grow their customer base.

CAC is commonly used by ecommerce businesses, subscription services, SaaS companies, agencies, startups and other organizations that invest money in attracting and converting new customers.

Simple example:

Imagine a business spends $8,500 on advertising, salaries, agency fees and marketing software in one month. If it acquires 100 new customers during the same period, its blended CAC is $85.

Customer Acquisition Cost Formula

The basic blended CAC formula is:

CAC = Total Acquisition Expenses ÷ Number of New Customers

Example using the calculator's sample figures:

CAC = $8,500 ÷ 100 = $85

This means the business spent an average of $85 to acquire each new customer during the selected measurement period.

What Counts as Acquisition Expenses?

The expenses included in CAC depend on your reporting method. A broader calculation may include several categories:

  • Advertising: Paid search, social media ads, display campaigns and other paid media.
  • Marketing salaries: Relevant compensation for employees working on customer acquisition.
  • Sales salaries: Relevant sales team compensation and acquisition-related commissions.
  • Agency fees: External marketing agencies, consultants and freelancers.
  • Software: CRM, marketing automation, analytics and sales tools.
  • Other expenses: Creative production, campaign-related events and other relevant costs.
Choose a consistent methodology

Some companies calculate paid-media CAC using advertising costs only. Others calculate blended CAC using broader sales and marketing expenses. Both can be useful, but they answer different questions. State clearly which expenses your calculation includes.

How to Calculate CAC Step by Step

  1. Choose a measurement period, such as one month or one quarter.
  2. Add the relevant acquisition-related marketing expenses.
  3. Add relevant sales expenses using a consistent allocation method.
  4. Count the new customers acquired during that period or attributable cohort.
  5. Divide total included expenses by the number of new customers.
  6. Compare the result with customer value, retention and profitability.

Blended CAC vs Paid CAC

Blended CAC

Blended CAC divides the selected total sales and marketing expenses by all new customers acquired in the measurement period. It provides a broader view of customer acquisition efficiency across channels.

Paid CAC

Paid CAC focuses on paid acquisition spending and the customers attributed to paid channels. Its calculation should use a clearly defined attribution method and matching customer count.

Do not compare paid CAC from one channel with blended CAC from the whole business as if they were identical metrics.

Understanding the CAC to LTV Relationship

Customer Lifetime Value (LTV) estimates the value or contribution generated by a customer over the relationship with a business. Comparing LTV with CAC can help businesses evaluate acquisition economics.

LTV to CAC Ratio = Customer Lifetime Value ÷ Customer Acquisition Cost

For example, if estimated customer lifetime value is $600 and CAC is $100, the LTV-to-CAC ratio is 6:1. This is a mathematical comparison, not a universal guarantee of profitability.

For a meaningful comparison, use compatible definitions. In particular, distinguish revenue-based LTV from gross-profit-based or contribution-margin-based LTV.

What Is a Good CAC?

There is no single CAC that is good for every business. A sustainable acquisition cost depends on product margins, customer lifetime value, retention, operating expenses, cash flow and the time required to recover acquisition spending.

When evaluating CAC, consider:

  • How much gross profit each new customer generates.
  • How frequently customers make repeat purchases.
  • How long customers remain subscribed or active.
  • How long it takes to recover acquisition spending.
  • Whether customers acquired from different channels have different quality.
  • Whether your calculation includes the full relevant acquisition cost.

How to Reduce Customer Acquisition Cost

Improve Conversion Rates

Review landing pages, checkout flows, forms, calls to action and mobile usability. Removing unnecessary friction can help more prospects become customers.

Improve Audience Targeting

Analyze which audiences generate valuable customers rather than simply large numbers of clicks or leads. Refine campaigns using reliable conversion and customer-quality data.

Optimize Sales Processes

Review lead qualification, follow-up times, sales handoffs and repetitive administrative tasks. Efficient processes can help teams use their resources more effectively.

Use Retention and Referrals

Customer retention and referral programs can support business growth. However, measure the costs of these programs separately and avoid treating every repeat purchase as a newly acquired customer.

Measure Channels Consistently

Track acquisition costs and customer quality by channel. Use consistent attribution rules, conversion definitions and reporting periods so comparisons remain meaningful.

Important CAC Calculation Limitations

  • Expense allocation can differ between companies.
  • Customers may convert weeks or months after the first marketing interaction.
  • Attribution systems may assign the same customer differently.
  • New customer counts should exclude existing customers and duplicate records.
  • Seasonality and one-time campaigns can cause short-term changes.
  • A blended CAC does not show the cost of every individual acquisition channel.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. What does CAC stand for?

CAC stands for Customer Acquisition Cost. It measures the average amount a business spends to acquire one new customer.

2. How do I calculate customer acquisition cost?

Add the relevant sales and marketing expenses for a defined period and divide the total by the number of new customers acquired during the matching period.

3. Should salaries be included in CAC?

A broader CAC calculation can include the relevant portion of sales and marketing salaries. Use a consistent allocation method and avoid including unrelated employee costs.

4. What is the difference between blended CAC and paid CAC?

Blended CAC includes the selected overall acquisition expenses and all relevant new customers. Paid CAC focuses on paid-channel costs and customers attributed to those channels.

5. Is a lower CAC always better?

Not necessarily. A lower CAC may be less valuable if it produces low-quality customers. Consider customer value, retention, margins and long-term profitability.

6. How often should I calculate CAC?

Many businesses monitor CAC monthly or quarterly. The best frequency depends on sales-cycle length, customer volume, seasonality and how quickly acquisition costs change.

7. What is the CAC to LTV ratio?

It compares estimated customer lifetime value with the cost of acquiring that customer. A business should use consistent value definitions and consider gross margins and retention.

8. Why can CAC increase even when advertising spend stays the same?

CAC can increase if fewer new customers are acquired, conversion rates decline, the sales cycle becomes longer or customer attribution changes.

9. Does this calculator include all business expenses?

No. It includes the expense categories you enter. You can add relevant acquisition expenses to the Other Acquisition Expenses field, but ordinary operating expenses should not automatically be treated as acquisition costs.

10. Can a small business use CAC?

Yes. Small businesses can use CAC to understand the cost of acquiring customers, compare marketing channels and plan growth spending. Even a simple, consistently measured estimate can be useful.
